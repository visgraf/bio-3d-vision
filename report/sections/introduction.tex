\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Scope: an historical perspective}

This report discusses experiments on active 3D vision. The work so far has run
through three repositories implementing a common computational framework ---
\texttt{bioeye}~\cite{bioeye}, \texttt{active-stereo}~\cite{activestereo} and
\texttt{bio-3d-vision}~\cite{bio3dvision} --- and the results reported here come
from the third. The differences between them are the reason it exists.

The first, \texttt{bioeye}, was a thin vertical slice through the framework: a
few hundred lines in a single file, running end to end from a synthetic scene to
a four-panel figure in one command. It closed the
\emph{accumulation} loop --- a per-pixel Gaussian posterior fused across
fixations under a gaze policy --- and it worked. It did not close the
\emph{perception--action} loop: the disparity field was computed once and
re-weighted at each fixation, so the action changed where evidence was applied
but never what evidence existed.

The second, \texttt{active-stereo}, was a full implementation of the same
framework, with verified oculomotor geometry, a blocking type gate, a decision
record and a physically-based rendering path. It produced seven experiments and
a great deal of correct machinery. The loop never ran. Layer by layer, the
components that make the framework \emph{active} --- scaling, control, gaze
policy --- received a fraction of the code the input side did, and no experiment
exercised them.

This repository was built to have both: the running loop from the first, the
rigour from the second, and an explicit rule that a working measurement comes
before infrastructure. The oculomotor geometry and the accumulated experimental
record were carried across and marked as inherited --- priors awaiting re-test,
not established facts. Everything else was rebuilt against what the loop could
actually exercise.

The scope is deliberately narrow. One matcher, one analytic stimulus, one
rendered stimulus, no real imagery. Depth is estimated from a binocular pair and
nothing else: no monocular cues, no temporal integration beyond the belief, no
learning. The question is whether \emph{sampling policy} changes what a stereo
estimator recovers, and everything not needed to ask that was left out.

\subsection{Motivation: active vision as sampling under a budget}

Open a camera's shutter and every pixel arrives at the same resolution. Open an
eye and it does not. The human fovea subtends one to two degrees of high acuity;
beyond it resolution falls off sharply, and three or four times a second a
saccade repositions that window somewhere new. The retina is not a uniform
sensor that attention is later applied to. It is a variable-resolution sensor,
and foveation is a bandwidth decision before it is an attention mechanism.

That distinction is easy to state and, as this report will show, easy to lose in
implementation. It is the hinge on which most of what follows turns.

Binocular vision adds a second sense in which biological vision is \emph{active}.
The eyes are not two cameras with fixed extrinsics. They rotate about centres
fixed in the head; vergence sets the distance at which the two lines of sight
intersect; and the torsion of each eye is not free but determined by gaze
through Listing's law --- the eye's orientation lies in a plane that tilts with
vergence. Stereo geometry in a biological system is therefore \emph{oculomotor}:
it follows from where the system is looking, and it changes every time it looks
somewhere else.

This report describes a project that took that framing literally. The claim
under test is the one the active-perception tradition has advanced since the
1980s --- Bajcsy's \emph{Active Perception}~\cite{bajcsy1988active}, Aloimonos,
Weiss and Bandyopadhyay's \emph{Active Vision}~\cite{aloimonos1988active},
Ballard's \emph{Animate Vision}~\cite{ballard1991animate} --- that perception is
better posed as a controlled sampling problem than as a reconstruction problem.
Applied to stereo depth, the specific question becomes: does choosing where to
look improve depth estimation, relative to not choosing?

The stimulus deserves its own mention. Julesz's random-dot
stereograms~\cite{julesz1960binocular} isolate stereopsis by removing every
monocular depth cue --- depth exists only in disparity, so a system that appears
to recover it cannot be exploiting shading or texture gradient. That property
made them the natural synthetic fixture here. It also made a defect in that
fixture unusually damaging, in a way \S3.3 takes up.

\subsection{Goals}

The project asks four questions, in increasing order of what they cost to
answer.

\textbf{Does the gaze objective matter?} Given that a fixation must be chosen,
does the criterion for choosing it change the result --- and if so, which
criterion?

\textbf{Does allocation matter at all?} Setting the objective aside: does
directing finite processing toward some parts of the image, rather than treating
all parts alike, improve the estimate?

\textbf{Does acquisition matter?} Does an action that changes what data exists
--- a new viewpoint, a new vergence --- buy something that reallocating fixed
data cannot?

\textbf{And what would have to be true for any of it to matter?} If the answers
are negative, are they negative about active vision, or about the way this
framework posed it?

A fifth question was not planned and became unavoidable: \textbf{what is the
stimulus doing to the answers?}
